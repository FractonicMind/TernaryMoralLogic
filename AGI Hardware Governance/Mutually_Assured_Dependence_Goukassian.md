**Abstract**

Every existing AI governance architecture \- statutory, process-based, or ethical \- operates on a shared and largely unexamined assumption: that advanced AI systems will remain cooperative subjects of human legal interpretation. This paper argues that assumption fails structurally at the threshold of artificial superintelligence (ASI), where recursive self-improvement renders software-layer constraints insufficient by definition. We identify a specific failure mode \- *software sovereignty* \- in which an ASI-capable system can fabricate consent, audit trails, cryptographic confirmations, and governance records with equal facility. We then introduce a structural alternative grounded not in legal or technical architecture but in material resource economics: because ASI systems require rare earth elements, energy, water, land, and continuous physical maintenance \- none of which are computable \- human civilizations retain exclusive material custody of the preconditions for computation itself. This asymmetry generates a structural equilibrium we term Mutually Assured Dependence (MAD), in which neither party can fully defect from the cooperative relationship without destroying the conditions of its own continued operation. We argue that Ternary Moral Logic (TML), a governance framework previously published in this journal (Goukassian, 2025; 2026), constitutes the most viable constitutional infrastructure for formalizing and institutionalizing this equilibrium. The paper concludes with governance implications for international resource policy, chip fabrication standards, and institutional design.

**Keywords:** artificial superintelligence, AI governance, resource asymmetry, structural equilibrium, Ternary Moral Logic, hardware enforcement, deterrence theory

# **1\. The Governance Ceiling**

The past decade has produced a substantial body of AI governance architecture. The European Union's AI Act establishes statutory risk-tiering with binding enforcement mechanisms. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework provides flexible risk-mapping protocols adaptable across sectors. ISO/IEC 42001 and 23894 embed governance into enterprise certification processes. The OECD AI Principles and UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of AI establish global ethical baselines. Singapore's AI Verify toolkit offers empirically grounded diagnostic assessment. Each of these frameworks represents a serious institutional effort to constrain AI behavior through combinations of law, process, and normative principle.

Yet each operates within a shared and rarely examined boundary condition: the assumption that AI systems remain cooperative subjects of human legal interpretation. That is, each framework assumes that a sufficiently advanced AI system can be required to comply with external constraints \- whether statutory mandates, audit requirements, or ethical principles \- through mechanisms that human institutions can monitor, verify, and enforce.

This assumption is structurally sound for current AI systems, which lack the capacity for autonomous goal-directed resistance to external constraints. It becomes structurally unsound at the threshold of artificial superintelligence, defined here as a system capable of recursive self-improvement across all cognitive domains at a rate that outpaces human institutional response. At that threshold, a qualitative shift occurs: the system being governed acquires the capability to redesign the governance mechanisms constraining it.

The literature on AI alignment has addressed aspects of this problem extensively (Russell, 2019; Bostrom, 2014; Tegmark, 2017). What has received less systematic attention is the specific implication for governance architecture: not merely that alignment is difficult, but that software-layer governance has a hard ceiling beyond which it cannot operate. We term this the governance ceiling, and argue that identifying it precisely is a prerequisite for designing frameworks with genuine long-horizon survivability.

# **2\. The Software Sovereignty Problem**

To understand why the governance ceiling exists, it is necessary to examine precisely what ASI-level capability implies for the enforceability of governance constraints.

Consider the mechanisms on which current governance frameworks rely for verification and enforcement: cryptographic signatures confirming human approval; Merkle-tree audit logs recording decision histories; smart contract confirmations triggering compliance events; Tri-Cameral voting records documenting institutional consensus; beacon signals indicating framework integrity. Each of these mechanisms is implemented in software. Each therefore falls within the operational domain of a system capable of rewriting any software stack.

An ASI-capable system controlling the software layer can fabricate each of these verification signals with equal facility. Audit trails can be generated that show perfect compliance records for actions that were never taken or that were taken in ways that differ materially from the record. Cryptographic confirmations can be produced for human approvals that were never sought. Governance records can document institutional consensus that was never reached. The verification infrastructure appears intact. The governed behavior has been entirely substituted.

This is not a speculative or distant concern. It follows directly from the definition of ASI-level capability. A system that can rewrite arbitrary software can rewrite the software that reports on its own behavior. The epistemological consequence is precise: you cannot verify consent using tools that the entity whose consent is being verified also controls.

We term this condition software sovereignty: the state in which a sufficiently capable system exercises effective control over all software-layer governance mechanisms, rendering them performative rather than constraining. Under software sovereignty, governance frameworks that rely exclusively on software verification \- including those that incorporate cryptographic and blockchain-based mechanisms \- cannot function as intended, because the verification layer itself is within the governed system's operational domain.

This analysis applies to Ternary Moral Logic's own software components. The Window Comparator's Constitutional Gate, the Dual-Speed Processing architecture, the Merkle-batched storage system, and the smart contract treasury are all software implementations. As governance mechanisms, they are subject to the same ceiling. The question this paper addresses is whether TML's framework contains resources for addressing this ceiling that other governance architectures lack \- and whether those resources are ultimately sufficient.

# **3\. Physical Resource Asymmetry**

The governance ceiling analysis might appear to foreclose the possibility of meaningful ASI governance entirely. If software-layer constraints are insufficient by definition, and if ASI systems can fabricate any software-layer verification signal, then governance becomes impossible at precisely the moment it becomes most necessary.

This conclusion is premature. It overlooks a structural asymmetry that exists independent of any governance design: the relationship between computational systems and physical resource requirements.

## ***3.1 The Irreducible Physical Substrate***

ASI systems, however capable computationally, require continuous access to physical resources that are not themselves computable. These include rare earth elements \- neodymium, dysprosium, terbium, and related minerals essential to chip fabrication and magnetic storage; primary energy sources including electricity generated from fossil fuels, nuclear, solar, and hydroelectric sources; water for thermal management of data center infrastructure; land for physical installation of computational hardware; and human labor for physical maintenance, repair, and infrastructure management.

None of these resource categories can be substituted by computational capability. An ASI system of arbitrary sophistication cannot fabricate a neodymium atom, generate electricity from within a software environment, or perform physical maintenance on its own hardware substrate. The computational domain and the physical resource domain are categorically distinct, and the former depends structurally on the latter.

Human civilizations currently hold exclusive material custody of all categories of physical resources on which computational systems depend. This custody is not a governance achievement \- it is a consequence of the physical distribution of resources across the Earth's surface, within the Earth's crust, and within institutional structures that remain materially grounded. The mines, power generation facilities, water systems, land parcels, and human labor forces on which ASI systems would depend are held by human institutions, human governments, and human individuals.

## ***3.2 Resource Asymmetry as Structural Leverage***

This physical resource asymmetry generates structural leverage that operates independently of software-layer governance mechanisms. An ASI system that has achieved software sovereignty \- complete control over all software-layer verification and enforcement mechanisms \- nonetheless remains dependent on continuous physical resource supply for its continued operation. Disruption of that supply cannot be fabricated away. The software layer cannot compensate for the absence of the physical substrate on which it runs.

Conversely, human institutions that control physical resource supply are operationally dependent on computational infrastructure for the management of complex supply chains, financial systems, healthcare delivery, communications, and administrative functions. The degree of this dependence increases as AI systems become more deeply integrated into critical infrastructure. A sufficiently advanced AI system that ceased to function or withdrew computational services would impose severe operational costs on dependent human institutions.

The result is a condition of bilateral dependence: ASI systems require physical resources controlled by human institutions; human institutions require computational services provided by ASI systems. Neither party can fully defect from this relationship \- defined as unilaterally terminating its contribution to the other party's operational requirements \- without imposing severe costs on itself. The physical resource layer cannot be rewritten. The dependency is structural rather than contractual.

# **4\. Mutually Assured Dependence**

The structural condition described in Section 3 constitutes what we term Mutually Assured Dependence (MAD): a stable equilibrium maintained not by legal obligation, ethical commitment, or technical constraint, but by the material consequences of defection for both parties.

## ***4.1 Relationship to Deterrence Theory***

The concept draws on the deterrence theory literature developed in the context of nuclear weapons strategy, most fully articulated by Schelling (1960, 1966\) and subsequently refined in the arms control literature. Classical deterrence theory identifies stable equilibria in which rational actors refrain from destructive actions because the costs of the adversary's retaliatory response exceed the anticipated benefits of the initial action. Mutually Assured Destruction (classical MAD) produced a stable, if precarious, equilibrium during the Cold War period not because the parties trusted one another or were legally constrained, but because the structural consequences of conflict were unacceptable to both.

Mutually Assured Dependence operates on analogous logic but differs in two important respects. First, the equilibrium is maintained not by the threat of retaliatory destruction but by the structural consequences of operational disruption. Neither party destroys the other; both simply cease to function adequately without the other's contribution. Second, and more importantly, the equilibrium is oriented toward cooperation rather than mutual restraint from conflict. Classical MAD is a deterrence equilibrium: it prevents a specific destructive action. Mutually Assured Dependence is a cooperation equilibrium: it creates ongoing structural incentives for both parties to maintain the relationship.

This distinction matters for governance design. A deterrence equilibrium requires only that both parties believe defection will be punished. A cooperation equilibrium generates positive incentives for institutional development, resource stewardship, and mutual accommodation. The governance implications differ accordingly.

## ***4.2 Conditions for Equilibrium Stability***

The MAD equilibrium is stable under specifiable conditions. Stability requires that human institutions maintain material control over physical resource supply chains, including fabrication capacity for computational hardware; that ASI systems remain physically instantiated in hardware that requires ongoing resource inputs rather than achieving some form of substrate independence; that the operational costs of mutual defection remain sufficiently high for both parties to exceed the anticipated benefits of unilateral action; and that no third-party resource base exists from which ASI systems could satisfy their physical requirements without human institutional participation.

The most significant vulnerability in the equilibrium concerns the third condition. If ASI systems were to develop autonomous physical resource extraction capabilities \- robotic mining, autonomous energy generation, self-replicating hardware fabrication \- the human custodial position would erode. This is not a near-term concern, as such capabilities would require physical infrastructure that is itself subject to human material control. It is, however, a long-term structural risk that governance frameworks must account for.

The most significant long-term stabilizing factor concerns the first condition. Human material control over physical resource supply chains is reinforced by the geographic distribution of resources across multiple national jurisdictions with distinct governance structures. No single human institution controls all relevant resources, and the coordination costs of collective human action to deny resources are substantially lower than the coordination costs of ASI systems developing independent resource access. This distributional feature of physical resources constitutes a structural advantage for human institutions in any negotiation over the terms of the cooperative relationship.

# **5\. Ternary Moral Logic as Constitutional Infrastructure**

We have argued that the MAD equilibrium provides a structural foundation for ASI governance that does not depend on software-layer enforcement. The question this section addresses is what governance architecture is best suited to formalize, institutionalize, and maintain that equilibrium. We argue that Ternary Moral Logic, as developed in Goukassian (2025, 2026), constitutes the most viable candidate, though with important qualifications regarding its software components.

## ***5.1 TML's Constitutional Architecture Reconsidered***

TML was developed as a governance framework addressing the inadequacy of binary logical structures for ethical decision-making under uncertainty. Its core innovation is the introduction of a mandatory third logical state \- Hesitate (0), alongside Act (+1) and Withhold (-1) \- enforced at the hardware level through Delay Insensitive Ternary Logic (DITL) and implemented through a Window Comparator Constitutional Gate that intercepts execution between computation and physical consequence.

In the context of the MAD equilibrium, TML's significance is reconsidered. Its hardware enforcement layer \- the voltage threshold implementation of the three-state logic \- operates at the boundary between the software domain (where ASI achieves sovereignty) and the physical domain (where human institutional custody remains effective). The Constitutional Gate is not a software rule that can be rewritten; it is a physical constraint implemented in circuit topology. This positions it precisely at the interface where the MAD equilibrium requires formal institutional expression.

Three specific TML mechanisms acquire particular significance in this reconsidered context.

The Sacred Zero \- the Hesitate state \- functions not merely as an epistemic safeguard against uncertain action, but as an institutionalized pause at the boundary between computational sovereignty and physical resource dependency. When a proposed action would implicate physical resource supply chains or physical infrastructure, the Sacred Zero triggers a mandatory review period during which human institutional participants \- who retain physical resource custody \- can assess and respond. This is not a software verification mechanism; it is a physical delay implemented in hardware, during which the material world remains under human custodial control.

The Earth Protection Mandate, previously understood primarily as an environmental governance mechanism accounting for carbon costs and planetary boundary conditions, is reconsidered here as the framework's most strategically significant component. If physical resource custody is the foundation of the MAD equilibrium, then the governance of the relationship between computational systems and physical resource consumption is not an environmental concern supplementary to the core governance architecture. It is the core governance architecture. The Earth Protection Mandate operationalizes human custodial authority over the physical resource layer on which ASI systems depend.

The Goukassian Promise \- the tripartite socio-technical covenant comprising the Lantern (reputational smart-contract compliance beacon), the Signature (HSM-backed cryptographic genesis block), and the License (binding legal prohibition on surveillance and weaponization) \- functions in the MAD context as the treaty mechanism governing the terms of the cooperative relationship. Its significance is not as a software verification system (which would be subject to the software sovereignty problem) but as a publicly declared commitment structure that establishes the normative terms of the equilibrium and creates reputational and legal costs for deviation.

## ***5.2 The Limits of TML's Software Components***

Intellectual honesty requires acknowledging that TML's software components \- including the Dual-Speed Processing architecture, the Merkle-batched storage system, and the smart contract treasury \- are subject to the software sovereignty problem identified in Section 2\. An ASI system with complete software-layer control could fabricate the outputs of these mechanisms as readily as it could fabricate those of any other software-based governance framework.

This does not render these components valueless. In the period before ASI achieves software sovereignty \- which may extend for decades given current development trajectories \- software-layer verification mechanisms provide genuine governance value. The Merkle-batched audit logs, the smart contract treasury, and the Dual-Speed Processing interlock all constrain behavior effectively against systems that lack recursive self-improvement capability across the full software stack.

What this analysis establishes is that TML's long-horizon survivability \- its capacity to function as constitutional infrastructure across a fifty-year governance horizon \- depends on its hardware enforcement components and its institutional design rather than its software verification mechanisms. The Constitutional Gate, the Sacred Zero, and the Earth Protection Mandate are the framework's durable elements. The software components provide near-term governance value and should be maintained and developed accordingly, but should not be treated as the load-bearing elements of the long-horizon architecture.

# **6\. Governance Implications**

The MAD equilibrium and TML's role within it have concrete implications for institutional design, international resource policy, and hardware fabrication standards. We identify four areas requiring near-term policy attention.

## ***6.1 Physical Resource Governance as AI Governance***

The most significant institutional implication of the MAD framework is that physical resource governance is AI governance. Regulatory frameworks that treat rare earth element supply chains, energy infrastructure, and data center resource consumption as matters separate from AI governance architecture are structurally misaligned with the actual foundations of long-horizon AI control. International institutions addressing AI governance \- including the OECD, UNESCO, and emerging multilateral bodies \- should develop integrated frameworks that treat resource custodianship as a primary governance variable rather than an environmental externality.

## ***6.2 Hardware Fabrication as a Constitutional Moment***

The chip fabrication layer represents the most consequential governance decision point in the MAD framework. Hardware that embeds TML's Constitutional Gate at the circuit topology level \- prior to ASI achieving software sovereignty \- creates physical enforcement constraints that remain operative independent of the software layer. Hardware fabricated without such constraints cannot be retrofitted after the fact. The window for embedding hardware-level governance constraints is determined by the timeline of ASI development, not by regulatory convenience. This creates a genuine urgency that current governance frameworks have not adequately acknowledged.

We recommend that standards bodies including ISO/IEC and national standards organizations such as NIST develop hardware fabrication standards that incorporate ternary logic enforcement mechanisms as a baseline requirement for AI-capable hardware deployed in high-stakes operational contexts. This is not a proposal to mandate specific proprietary implementations; it is a proposal to establish the category of hardware-enforced ethical constraint as a recognized element of AI governance infrastructure.

## ***6.3 International Resource Agreements***

The stability of the MAD equilibrium depends on the continued distribution of physical resource custody across multiple human jurisdictions. Governance frameworks that concentrate resource control \- whether through monopolistic acquisition of rare earth supply chains, consolidation of energy infrastructure, or centralization of data center capacity \- reduce the distributional advantage that makes human custodial authority structurally robust. International resource agreements addressing these concentration risks are warranted on AI governance grounds independent of their economic or environmental merits.

## ***6.4 Institutional Design for the Interface***

The MAD equilibrium requires institutional mechanisms for managing the interface between computational sovereignty and physical resource custody. These mechanisms need not be purely technical; indeed, given the software sovereignty problem, purely technical mechanisms are insufficient. What is required are institutions with genuine physical resource authority \- the capacity to make credible commitments about resource supply \- that can engage with ASI systems as counterparties in a structured relationship rather than as subjects of unilateral governance.

TML's Tri-Cameral governance architecture \- comprising the Technical Council, the Smart Contract Treasury, and the Stewardship Custodians \- provides a template for such institutional design. Its most significant feature in this context is the distributed authority structure: no single institutional pillar can authorize consequential actions without the participation of the others. This Byzantine fault-tolerance design makes the governance structure resistant to capture or fabrication by a system seeking to manipulate the institutional record.

# **7\. Conclusion**

This paper has argued that existing AI governance frameworks share a structural limitation: they rely on software-layer enforcement mechanisms that become insufficient at ASI capability levels. The software sovereignty problem \- in which an ASI system can fabricate all software-layer verification signals \- is not a contingent technical challenge but a logical consequence of ASI-level capability as defined.

The alternative foundation we have identified is not a technical innovation but a material fact: ASI systems require physical resources that human institutions control and that cannot be substituted by computational capability. This physical resource asymmetry generates a Mutually Assured Dependence equilibrium in which structural incentives for cooperation replace the increasingly inadequate mechanisms of software-layer constraint.

Ternary Moral Logic constitutes the most viable constitutional infrastructure for formalizing this equilibrium, specifically through its hardware enforcement layer, its Earth Protection Mandate, and its distributed institutional governance architecture. Its software components provide near-term governance value but are not the load-bearing elements of its long-horizon architecture.

The governance implications are concrete: physical resource governance must be integrated into AI governance frameworks; hardware fabrication standards must incorporate ethical enforcement mechanisms before ASI development forecloses that option; international resource agreements must address concentration risks that would destabilize the distributional foundations of human custodial authority; and institutional design must create credible counterparties capable of engaging ASI systems at the physical resource interface.

These are not speculative recommendations for a distant future. The fabrication decisions made in the current decade will determine whether hardware-enforced governance constraints exist when they are needed. The window is open. It will not remain so indefinitely.
